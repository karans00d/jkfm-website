Strata Cleaning Standards: What Your Common Areas Say About Your Building

Common Areas · Published 06/04/2026 · Updated 24/07/2026

Common areas

Residents and buyers judge a building within seconds of walking through the door. A clean, well maintained lobby signals that the committee takes the property seriously. Worn carpets, dusty corners and smudged lift doors suggest neglect, even if the rest of the building is fine. The trouble for a committee is that "the cleaning seems fine" and "the cleaning is fine" are different claims, and most committees only test the first one. Here is a way to test the second.

Walk your own building: a twenty minute standards check

You do not need a cleaning background. You need twenty minutes, your phone camera, and the route a visitor would take. Cleaning quality shows at edges, undersides and high points, because surfaces in the middle of a room get wiped even by a poor clean. The edges only get done by a systematic one.

In the lobby:

  • a.Entry glass and door frames at eye level. Fingermarks here are hours old, not weeks, but a film of grime in the corners of the glass is a schedule problem.
  • b.Floor edges and corners, where dust ribbons collect first.
  • c.Mailbox tops, light switches and door handles.
  • d.Ceiling corners and light fittings: cobwebs and dead insects.
  • e.The entry mat. Lift a corner and look under it.
  • f.Under and behind any furniture. Move one chair.

In the lifts:

  • a.Stainless doors and panels, inside and out. Smudge free at handover each morning is a reasonable standard.
  • b.The door tracks. Run a finger along the runner grooves; grit collects there and a good clean vacuums it out.
  • c.Mirror, buttons and handrail.
  • d.Floor edges and the gap where floor meets wall.
  • e.The light diffuser overhead.

In the bin room:

  • a.The smell test at the door. A bin room smells on collection day. One that smells midweek is missing its wash downs.
  • b.The floor around and under the bins, not just the walkway.
  • c.Bin lids and handles.
  • d.Floor drains: clear, with the grate in place.
  • e.Walls at bin height, where impact marks and splash accumulate.
  • f.Any sign of pests.

Score nothing. Just photograph what you find. Ten photos from this walk tell you more about your cleaning contract than a year of assuming.

Setting and maintaining standards

Define what clean means for your building: frequency, scope and expected condition, in writing. A clear cleaning schedule across daily, weekly and periodic tasks keeps standards consistent. The same team, the same checklist, the same outcome. Periodic tasks are where contracts quietly fail, because a missed high dust or skipped bin room wash down is invisible for weeks. If your cleaning specification does not state how often those happen, that is the first thing to fix, and the checklist above is a reasonable starting scope. When cleaning is integrated with facilities management, nothing falls through the cracks, because the person checking the work also answers for the building.

What documented reporting looks like

A cleaning report should show its working. "All areas cleaned as per schedule" is a sentence, and a sentence is not evidence. Documented reporting means:

  • a.A dated, signed checklist for every shift, tied to the schedule, so each task the schedule promises is ticked off by name on the day it was done.
  • b.Periodic tasks recorded when they happen. If the schedule says the bin room is washed down weekly, the record shows the date of each wash down, and a missed week is visible instead of invisible.
  • c.Issues flagged with a location, and photographed where an issue warrants it, so a burnt out globe or damaged wall becomes a maintenance item the same day the cleaner spots it, not a discovery at the next inspection.
  • d.A monthly summary the committee can scan in five minutes, tied to the schedule so gaps are visible.

This costs the cleaner a few seconds per task and costs the committee nothing. What it buys is the end of the fine versus not fine argument, because both sides are looking at the same records. It also builds the documentation trail that serves the building well beyond cleaning.

The payoff

Better resident satisfaction, stronger first impressions for prospective buyers, and a building that feels cared for. Cleaning is one of the most visible ways a committee can improve quality of life for everyone, and one of the few building services every single resident experiences every single day. A committee that can walk its own building with a checklist, and a cleaner whose work is documented shift by shift, between them close the gap where standards slide.
